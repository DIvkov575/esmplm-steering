\title{Validating Causal Steering in Protein Language Models: \\
Proxy Accuracy Does Not Guarantee Steering Success}

\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Activation steering changes model output by modifying internal
activations. We ask whether agreement between a scoring proxy and
experimental labels also indicates that the proxy will provide a useful
steering target. We test five protein properties in ESM2-650M with
difference-of-means steering vectors and a fixed six-requirement
evaluation protocol. The binding-affinity proxy has the strongest
held-out correlation in the study ($r=0.80$), but its steering direction
has no measurable effect. A weaker catalytic-activity proxy ($r=0.22$)
produces an effect that repeats across three random seeds. We find a
similar mismatch for individual attention heads: strong correlation with
known 3D contacts does not imply a large effect under ablation. Two
additional checks change how we interpret other results. A
residue-exclusion check gives different conclusions across seeds, and an
apparent expression-yield effect uses a direction that overlaps with the
intrinsic-disorder direction. These results show that proxy validation
tests the score, while direct intervention tests are needed to evaluate
the steering direction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Difference-of-means activation steering modifies a model's residual
stream during generation. The steering direction is obtained by
subtracting the mean activation of a low-property group from the mean
activation of a high-property group. This method has reproduced a known
thermostability effect in ESM2-650M \citep{huang2025steering}.

That result leaves an important question open when the method is applied
to a new protein property. A strong correlation between a scoring proxy
and experimental labels shows that the score contains information about
the property. However, it does not show that a difference-of-means vector
built from that property will change the model in the intended way. Four
of the five properties in this study reach the steering stage. The order
of their proxy correlations does not match the order of their steering
effects.

We test a related assumption at the level of individual model components.
An attention head that correlates strongly with known protein contacts
is not unusually important when it is ablated. Together, these
experiments distinguish evidence that a model represents a property from
evidence that a particular intervention controls that property.

\section{Related Work}

\citet{huang2025steering} introduced difference-of-means activation
steering for protein language models and validated the method on
thermostability. We apply the same intervention to five new properties
and test whether proxy accuracy predicts steering success.

\citet{vig2021bertology} identified attention heads whose attention
patterns correlate with 3D protein contacts. The original study
described this evidence as associative. We reproduce their analysis,
test the most enriched head in our data by causal ablation, and extend
the analysis to all 480 heads in the model.

\citet{adams2025mechanistic} used linear probes to identify
thermostability and localization information in sparse-autoencoder
features from ESM-2. Their steering experiment tested whether a selected
latent produced more coherent sequence changes than a random latent. It
did not compare the result with an experimental property label. Our
study addresses a related but different question: whether correlational
evidence predicts the result of a downstream intervention.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Steering setup}

For each property, we form a steering vector from the mean activation
difference between high-property and low-property sequence groups. The
vector is added to ESM2-650M's residual stream during generation. We
evaluate the resulting sequences with a property-specific compositional
proxy that was checked against experimental labels before steering. Each
property-derived steering direction is compared with a random direction
of the same norm.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

We fix six requirements before testing the new properties. First, the
property-derived direction must outperform the matched-norm random
direction in a paired bootstrap test with 10,000 resamples. Second, the
effect must show a monotonic dose response across at least three
intervention strengths within a range where generation remains stable.
Third, the effect must remain after the most frequently substituted
residues are excluded from the score. This residue-exclusion check tests
whether the result depends only on a narrow change in amino-acid
composition. Fourth, the scoring proxy must agree with experimental
labels on held-out data. Fifth, the method must outperform an existing
baseline when a suitable baseline is available. Sixth, an evaluation of
a new property must contain at least 150 held-out sequences.

We use three assessment outcomes. If proxy validation fails, we reject
the proxy and do not attempt steering. If the main steering test
succeeds but residue exclusion fails or changes across seeds, we
classify the evidence as inconclusive. We call a result supported only
when it satisfies every applicable requirement.

\subsection{Attention-head ablation}

We also test whether contact correlation predicts the importance of an
individual attention head. Following \citet{vig2021bertology}, we select
the most contact-enriched head in our data, ablate it, and measure the
change in masked-residue prediction. We then repeat the ablation for all
480 heads in the model.

\subsection{Binding affinity}

We use a ProteinGym deep-mutational-scanning assay
\citep{notin2023proteingym} of KRAS binding to a DARPin. The proxy is
built from position-specific mutational-sensitivity weights learned on
the training split. It reaches $r=0.80$ on held-out data. The result is
supported by a weight-shuffle control ($r=-0.001$), evaluation on
positions excluded from weight fitting ($r=0.54$), and extrapolation
from single to double mutants ($r=0.70$).

Despite this validation, the steering direction has no detectable effect
at any tested strength. Generation remains stable, so output collapse
does not explain the result.

\subsection{Catalytic activity}

For catalytic activity, we use a cross-protein enzyme turnover dataset
from DLKcat \citep{li2022dlkcat}. The proxy measures the fraction of
glycine minus the fraction of arginine. Its held-out correlation is only
$r=0.22$, the weakest among the four targets that reach the steering
stage. Even so, the steering effect is monotonic, remains significant
after residue exclusion, and repeats across three random seeds.

\subsection{Intrinsic disorder}
\label{sec:disorder}

We use DisProt annotations \citep{quaglia2022disprot}. The
intrinsic-disorder proxy uses the published TOP-IDP amino-acid scale
\citep{campen2008topidp}. It reaches held-out $r=0.45$ and per-residue
AUC $0.71$. The steering effect is significant and monotonic on all
three seeds. The residue-exclusion check is less stable: it passes on
two seeds but fails on the third. The main effect is therefore
repeatable, but one artifact check is not.

\subsection{Immunogenicity and expression yield}

For immunogenicity, we use records from the Immune Epitope Database
\citep{vita2019iedb}. Candidate proxies correlate with a surrogate
binding-affinity endpoint but fall to $r\leq0.10$ on the measured
T-cell-response endpoint. One full-length-antigen proxy reaches
$r=0.38$, but organism identity explains 58\% of the apparent signal.
When organism identity is held out during cross-validation, the
correlation changes to $-0.32$. Because no proxy passes the validation
requirement, we do not run a steering experiment.

We use expression-yield measurements from eSol
\citep{niwa2009esol}. The proxy reaches $r=0.31$--$0.34$ and produces a
significant dose response. The effect disappears under residue
exclusion, so the protocol classifies the result as inconclusive.

\subsection{Attention-head ablation}

The most contact-enriched head in our replication has 12.9-fold
enrichment over background. Ablating it produces a change that is
statistically indistinguishable from ablating a low-correlation control
head. The full sweep does not provide a precise rank because the
evaluation uses 104 sampled positions and many heads have identical
effects. The selected head belongs to a tied block of 132 heads spanning
ranks 286--417. Strong contact correlation therefore does not imply a
large or unique contribution to masked-residue prediction.

\section{Failure Analysis and Reliability Checks}
\label{sec:failure}

\subsection{The binding-affinity groups differ at few residues}

The binding-affinity sequences are point mutants of one 188-residue
reference protein. The high- and low-binding groups therefore differ by
only one or two residues per sequence. Their mean per-residue
compositional distance is $0.003$, compared with $0.02$--$0.05$ for the
three cross-protein datasets. The proxy uses position-specific weights,
so it can predict binding labels from a small number of mutations. In
contrast, the steering direction averages activations over groups of
nearly identical sequences. The few mutations that distinguish the
groups may contribute too little to that average. This is a possible
explanation for the failed intervention, not a demonstrated mechanism.

\subsection{The expression-yield direction overlaps with disorder}

We compare the expression-yield steering vector with every other target
vector. Its cosine similarity with the intrinsic-disorder direction is
$+0.38$ across all 33 layers and rises to $+0.56$--$0.67$ in the three
deepest layers. This overlap shows that the directions are not orthogonal
and may capture some of the same compositional variation. It does not
show that disorder causes the expression-yield result. Because the
expression-yield result also fails residue exclusion, we do not count it
as separate evidence for a new steering effect.

\subsection{Checks that changed the interpretation}

An early thermostability comparison selected the best intervention
strength from a range that included unstable generation. At the selected
strength, the 33-layer intervention had already collapsed, while a
5-layer intervention had not. The 5-layer version appeared better only
because it failed at a higher strength. After we restricted the
comparison to a stable range and reran the experiment, the 5-layer
intervention retained 43--59\% of the full effect.

The intrinsic-disorder experiment revealed a different problem. The main
effect repeats across all three seeds, but the residue-exclusion check
passes on only two. A single run could therefore give a misleading
verdict about that criterion.

\section{Discussion}

These results show why representation and intervention should be
evaluated separately. A proxy can accurately summarize experimental
labels without defining a direction that changes model output in the
intended way. Likewise, an attention head can align with a biological
pattern without making a large, individually measurable contribution to
the model's prediction.

Proxy validation remains necessary because it rules out scores that do
not track the target, as the immunogenicity experiment shows. The problem
arises when evidence about the score is also treated as evidence about
the steering vector. The binding-affinity experiment shows that the score
and the vector can depend on different sources of signal.

The experiments suggest three practical checks. Before constructing a
steering vector, researchers should measure how much the high- and
low-property groups differ. Comparisons between interventions should use
the same range of strengths, and every intervention should produce
stable sequences throughout that range. Important results should be
repeated across seeds, including the artifact checks used to support
them.

\section{Limitations}

All experiments use ESM2-650M at one model scale. The proxies are
compositional heuristics rather than learned predictors. A stronger
non-compositional proxy could behave differently. We selected each final
proxy from several candidates using the same held-out split on which we
report its correlation, so the reported correlations are likely
optimistic.

We ran multiple significance tests without a formal correction. Requiring
agreement across intervention strengths and seeds reduces this risk but
does not remove it. Seed robustness was checked for only two targets.
We also did not evaluate generated sequences with a wet-lab assay or a
structural plausibility model. A successful protocol result means that
the intervention changes a validated proxy. It does not establish that
the generated protein has the intended biological property.

\section{Conclusion}

Across these experiments, higher proxy accuracy does not guarantee more
successful steering. The binding-affinity result provides the clearest
counterexample, and the attention-head ablation shows a related mismatch
at the level of a model component. Correlational evidence can show that a
model represents a property, but direct intervention tests are still
needed before that representation is used to guide steering.

\section*{Responsible-use statement}
This work studies errors in the evaluation of activation steering for
protein language models. It does not introduce a new method for
generating harmful biological sequences. The target properties are
standard protein-engineering objectives, and the experiments use a
previously published steering method. We do not identify risks beyond
those already associated with protein language models and standard
protein-engineering research.
