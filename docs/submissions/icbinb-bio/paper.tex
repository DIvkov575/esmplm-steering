\title{Failure Modes in Causal Steering of Protein Language Models: \\
Why Proxy Accuracy Does Not Guarantee Steering Success}

\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Difference-of-means activation steering has reproduced a known
thermostability effect in ESM2-650M. We test whether the same method
transfers to five new protein properties and whether a more accurate
scoring proxy provides a better steering target. The binding-affinity
proxy has the highest held-out correlation in the study ($r=0.80$), but
its steering direction has no measurable effect. A weaker
catalytic-activity proxy ($r=0.22$) produces a consistent effect across
three random seeds. An immunogenicity proxy that initially appears
predictive is explained by source-organism confounding, so that target
does not reach the steering stage. An expression-yield effect fails a
residue-exclusion check and overlaps with the steering direction for
intrinsic disorder. The evaluation also exposes two problems in our own
analysis. One comparison used an intervention strength at which a
baseline had already collapsed, while one robustness criterion changed
its verdict across seeds. These cases show why proxy validation and a
single successful run are not sufficient evidence for a reliable
steering intervention.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Research question and setting}

Difference-of-means activation steering adds a direction to a protein
language model's residual stream during generation. The direction is
formed from the difference between mean activations for examples with
high and low values of a target property. The method has reproduced a
known thermostability effect in ESM2-650M
\citep{huang2025steering}.

Applying the method to a new property raises two separate questions. A
scoring proxy may agree with experimental labels, but the steering
direction built for that property may still be ineffective. A result may
also depend on the train-test split, random control direction, or
intervention strength. We therefore evaluate both the steering direction
and the stability of the evidence used to support it.

\subsection{Targets and measurements}

The study covers five properties that were not part of the original
thermostability experiment: binding affinity, catalytic activity,
intrinsic disorder, immunogenicity, and expression yield. For each
property, we first measure the Pearson correlation between a
compositional proxy and experimental labels on held-out data. If the
proxy passes this check, we compare the property-derived steering
direction with a random direction of the same norm.

The main outcome is the score difference between the learned and random
directions. We also test whether the effect changes monotonically with
intervention strength, remains after dominant residue substitutions are
excluded from scoring, and repeats across random seeds. If proxy
accuracy were a useful way to select steering targets, properties with
stronger held-out correlations would also have larger or more consistent
steering effects. We do not observe that pattern.

\section{Proposed Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

\subsection{Steering procedure}

For each target, we divide real protein sequences into high-property and
low-property groups. We compute the mean activation for each group at
every layer of ESM2-650M and subtract the two means. During generation,
we add a scaled version of this direction to the residual stream. The
same code handles vector construction, random controls, sequence
generation, bootstrap testing, and output filtering for every target.
Using one implementation allows us to change the dataset and scoring
proxy without changing the intervention or evaluation procedure.

\subsection{Six evaluation requirements}

We fix the following requirements before testing the new properties:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The property-derived direction must outperform a random direction
  of the same norm in a paired bootstrap test with 10,000 resamples.
\item The effect must increase monotonically across at least three
  intervention strengths in a range where generation remains stable.
\item The effect must remain after the most frequently substituted
  residues are excluded from the score.
\item The scoring proxy must agree with experimental labels on held-out
  data before steering is attempted.
\item The method must outperform an existing baseline when a suitable
  baseline is available.
\item An evaluation of a new property must contain at least 150 held-out
  sequences.
\end{enumerate}

The third requirement checks for compositional collapse. A steering
direction can appear successful if it repeatedly inserts one residue
that directly increases the proxy score. Excluding the dominant
substitutions tests whether the effect extends beyond that narrow change.
We use three assessment outcomes. If proxy validation fails, we reject
the proxy and do not attempt steering. If the main steering test
succeeds but residue exclusion fails or changes across seeds, we
classify the evidence as inconclusive. We call a result supported only
when it satisfies every applicable requirement.

\subsection{Attention-head comparison}

We also examine whether the same problem appears at the level of
individual attention heads. Following \citet{vig2021bertology}, we
measure contact enrichment in their protein language model and select
the most enriched head in our data. We ablate that head and compare the
change in masked-residue prediction with a low-correlation control head.
We then repeat the ablation for all 480 heads in the model.

\subsection{Binding affinity}

We use a ProteinGym deep-mutational-scanning assay
\citep{notin2023proteingym} of KRAS binding to a DARPin. The proxy uses
position-specific mutational-sensitivity weights learned on the training
split and reaches $r=0.80$ on held-out data. A weight-shuffle control
gives $r=-0.001$. The proxy also generalizes to positions excluded from
weight fitting ($r=0.54$) and from single to double mutants ($r=0.70$).

The steering direction nevertheless has no detectable effect at any
tested strength. Because generation remains stable and produces no
degenerate sequences at those strengths, output collapse does not
explain the result.

\subsection{Catalytic activity}

For catalytic activity, we use a cross-protein enzyme turnover dataset
from DLKcat \citep{li2022dlkcat}. The proxy measures the fraction of
glycine minus the fraction of arginine. Its held-out correlation is
$r=0.22$, the weakest among the four targets that reach the steering
stage. Even so, the steering effect is significant and monotonic,
remains after residue exclusion, and repeats across three random seeds.

\subsection{Intrinsic disorder}
\label{sec:disorder}

We use DisProt annotations \citep{quaglia2022disprot}. The
intrinsic-disorder proxy uses the published TOP-IDP amino-acid scale
\citep{campen2008topidp}. It reaches held-out $r=0.45$ and per-residue
AUC $0.71$. The main steering effect is significant and monotonic on all
three random seeds. The residue-exclusion check passes on two seeds but
fails on the third. The effect is repeatable, but the evidence against a
simple compositional artifact is not equally strong on every seed.

\subsection{Immunogenicity}

For immunogenicity, we use records from the Immune Epitope Database
\citep{vita2019iedb}. Candidate proxies correlate with a surrogate
binding-affinity endpoint but fall to $r\leq0.10$ on the measured
T-cell-response endpoint. One proxy for full-length antigens initially
reaches $r=0.38$. Further testing shows that organism identity explains
58\% of this signal. When organism identity is held out during
cross-validation, the correlation changes to $-0.32$. Because no proxy
passes the validation requirement, we do not run a steering experiment.

\subsection{Expression yield}

We use expression-yield measurements from eSol
\citep{niwa2009esol}. The proxy reaches $r=0.31$--$0.34$ and produces a
significant dose response. The effect disappears when the dominant
residue substitutions are excluded. The main result is therefore
inconclusive under the fixed protocol.

\subsection{Attention-head ablation}

In our replication of \citet{vig2021bertology}, the most
contact-enriched head has 12.9-fold enrichment over background. Ablating
it produces a change that is statistically indistinguishable from
ablating a low-correlation control head. The full sweep does not provide
a precise rank because the evaluation uses 104 sampled positions and
many heads have identical measured effects. The selected head shares its
value with 131 other heads, forming a tied block that spans ranks
286--417. At the same resolution, 166 heads show no measurable ablation
effect. Strong contact correlation therefore does not imply a large or
unique contribution to masked-residue prediction.

\section{Reason for Failure}
\label{sec:failure}

\subsection{The binding-affinity groups differ at few residues}

The binding-affinity sequences are point mutants of one 188-residue
reference protein. The high- and low-binding groups differ by only one
or two residues per sequence. Their mean per-residue compositional
distance is $0.003$, compared with $0.02$--$0.05$ for the three
cross-protein datasets.

This difference offers a possible explanation for the failed
intervention. The proxy uses position-specific weights, so it can predict
binding labels from a small number of mutations. In contrast, the
steering direction averages activations over groups of nearly identical
sequences. The few mutations that distinguish the groups may contribute
too little to that average. Our evidence supports this explanation as a
possibility, not as a demonstrated mechanism.

\subsection{The expression-yield direction overlaps with disorder}

We compare the expression-yield steering vector with every other target
vector. Its cosine similarity with the intrinsic-disorder direction is
$+0.38$ across all 33 layers and rises to $+0.56$--$0.67$ in the three
deepest layers. This overlap shows that the directions are not orthogonal
and may capture some of the same compositional variation. It does not
show that disorder causes the expression-yield result. Because the
expression-yield result also fails residue exclusion, we do not count it
as separate evidence for a new steering effect. Vector similarity is
inexpensive to compute and is useful when several related targets are
studied in the same model.

\subsection{Unstable generation made five-layer steering look stronger}

In a follow-up thermostability experiment, we compared steering all 33
layers with steering only five layers previously identified as
important. An early analysis selected the best intervention strength
from the full tested range. At that strength, generation under the
33-layer intervention had already collapsed, while generation under the
5-layer intervention had not. The 5-layer version appeared better only
because it failed at a higher strength.

We restricted the comparison to a range where both interventions
produced stable sequences and reran the experiment. The 5-layer
intervention then retained 43--59\% of the full effect. This is a useful
partial result, but it does not support the original claim that five
layers perform as well as all 33.

\subsection{A robustness criterion changed across seeds}

The intrinsic-disorder effect repeats across all three seeds, but its
residue-exclusion check passes on only two. A single run could therefore
support either a pass or a failure for that criterion. Repeating only
the main effect is not enough when a separate artifact check is used to
justify the result.

\subsection{Practical recommendations}

Before steering, researchers should measure how much the high- and
low-property groups differ, not only how well the scoring proxy predicts
labels. Comparisons between methods or layer sets should use a range in
which every intervention produces stable output. Artifact checks should
be repeated across seeds when they support an important claim. When
several properties are studied together, similarities between their
steering vectors can reveal that two apparent effects may not be
independent.

\section{Related Work}

\citet{huang2025steering} introduced difference-of-means activation
steering for protein language models and validated the method on
thermostability. We apply the same intervention to five new properties
under one fixed evaluation protocol.

\citet{vig2021bertology} identified attention heads whose attention
patterns correlate with 3D protein contacts. We reproduce their analysis,
test the most enriched head in our data by causal ablation, and extend
the analysis to all heads in the model.

\citet{adams2025mechanistic} used linear probes to identify biological
information in sparse-autoencoder features from ESM-2. Their steering
experiment tested sequence coherence rather than agreement with an
experimental property label. Our study asks whether correlational
evidence predicts the outcome of a downstream intervention.

\section{Limitations}

All experiments use ESM2-650M at one model scale. The proxies are
compositional heuristics rather than learned predictors. A stronger
non-compositional proxy could behave differently. We selected each final
proxy from several candidates using the same held-out split on which we
report its correlation, so the reported correlations are likely
optimistic.

We ran multiple significance tests without a formal correction.
Agreement across intervention strengths and seeds reduces this risk but
does not remove it. Seed robustness was checked for only two targets. We
also did not evaluate generated sequences with a wet-lab assay or a
structural plausibility model. A successful protocol result means that
the intervention changes a validated proxy. It does not establish that
the generated protein has the intended biological property.

\section{Conclusion}

Across these experiments, higher proxy accuracy does not guarantee more
successful steering. The binding-affinity result shows that a
well-validated score can still produce an ineffective
difference-of-means direction. The seed and intervention-range checks
also show how a plausible result can receive the wrong interpretation.
Reliable steering claims require direct tests of the intervention, its
artifacts, and its stability.
